If you’d told me a year ago that I’d build an NL2SQL system from scratch I
would’ve smiled, nodded, and quietly changed the topic. But then a company
project landed in my lap, I said “sure, why not,
” and suddenly I was knee￾deep in embeddings, vector search, agentic reasoning, and a surprising
amount of philosophical reflection about context.
This blog is a mix of what I built, what I learned, and what I broke along the
way. It’s my first Medium post, so think of it as an honest engineering story
with a bit of humor sprinkled in.
How It Started: With Vanna, RAG, and Blissful Ignorance
Before I jump into what I built, it’s worth stepping back and asking: what
even is NL2SQL, and why does it matter?
Back in college, my idea of working with SQL was hilariously simple: spin up
a small database, create ten tables, add a hundred rows to each, write a few
SELECT queries, and call it a day.
But real-world data is nothing like that. In industry, databases don’t contain
ten tables; they contain hundreds. Some have millions of rows or thousands
of columns spread across multiple schemas, each representing a different
part of the business such as orders, shipments, inventory, quality,
forecasting, and planning.
Somewhere inside all that chaos lies incredibly rich business insight — but I
had no way to reach it directly.
That’s where NL2SQL comes in: the idea that you should be able to talk to
your data the way you talk to a data engineer. The problem? You can’t just
copy-paste all those tables, columns, and constraints into one massive GPT
prompt and say “figure it out.”
So NL2SQL became my way of bridging that gap: taking natural language and
translating it into queries that can actually operate on these massive,
production-scale datasets.
Figure 0: Naive Approach
When I started the project, I had:
Zero experience with NL2SQL
A surface-level understanding of how RAG worked
A probably unjustified amount of confidence
And the comforting thought: “How complicated could it be?”
The Version 0 of NL2SQL was hilariously naive, take the user’s question,
dump the entire schema, throw in some context, smash everything into a
single mega-prompt, and pray that GPT would magically produce the right
SQL.
And technically… it worked.
Sometimes.
If the question was simple enough and if the schema wasn’t too big.
But it was also painfully obvious why this approach sucked. The prompt was
too large, the reasoning was too shallow, and GPT would hallucinate
columns, misunderstand joins, or forget half the schema because it simply
couldn’t process everything in one go. The moment queries got even slightly
complex, the entire “one-prompt solution” collapsed.
That’s when I realized I needed something smarter, something retrieval￾based, schema-aware, and capable of narrowing context automatically.
Enter Vanna the open-source Text-to-SQL engine.
Figure 1: Initial single-shot RAG pipeline
Vanna (a public, MIT-licensed Python framework for NL2SQL built on RAG
[Retrieval-Augmented Generation]) works in two steps: first you feed it your
database schema, documentation, and optionally past SQL examples to build
a knowledge base; then you ask your questions in natural language.
Vanna retrieves only the relevant metadata + docs, invokes a large language
model, and outputs SQL tailored to your database which you can then run
directly.
So the first version of the RAG based approach was relatively simple: use
Vanna to translate natural language to SQL.
And it worked.
Well… until it didn’t.
The moment real business users joined the party, the questions escalated
from:
“Show me all customers.”
to the far more dramatic:
“Give me a region-wise breakdown of Q4 buyer order performance across
all active buyers with more than 10,000 units shipped, compare it with last
year, and explain why shipped quantities dipped — for example due to low
stock or delayed fulfillment.”
Vanna, being the polite model she is, tried her absolute best, but this was
basically asking a bicycle to tow a truck.
And honestly, at first I didn’t think I needed something more powerful. I
tried fixing it the “normal” way: adding better SQL examples to the context,
tightening domain rules, giving the model clearer instructions. I kept
tweaking, tuning, nudging… hoping it would somehow handle those harder
edge cases.
It didn’t.
The Great Pivot: Enter the Agentic “Thinking Mode”
Figure 2: The improved agentic pipeline
I didn’t start this project thinking I’d build a multi-agent system.
I didn’t even fully understand how agentic workflows operated in practice.
All I knew was that they were supposed to be powerful…
When Vanna began struggling with more complex buyer-order scenarios, it
became clear that a single-shot RAG approach wasn’t going to cut it. So I split
the intents:
General questions → answered directly
Metadata questions → ask the schema
Data questions → routed to Vanna
And here’s where the big change came in:
Within the Data intent, I added a user toggle.
By default, Vanna tries to answer with RAG. But if the user switches on
Thinking Mode, the system escalates the query into a deeper, agentic
workflow.
And suddenly, without planning for it, I found myself neck-deep in agents,
tools, intermediate reasoning, message passing, retries, context juggling,
and the gentle art of preventing the LLM from spiraling into chaos — all
things I didn’t know a month earlier.
But surprisingly? I loved it.
What started as a tiny experimental feature — something I built during a
hackathon with embarrassingly low expectations — became one of the most
powerful parts of the system. My “let’s just try this for fun” agentic mode
ended up generating 70+ line SQL queries correctly on day one.
I stared at my laptop like: “…wait… it can do that?”
That was the moment I realized this wasn’t just a backup plan.
It was the thinking mode of the application — the same way GPT has a
“normal mode” and a “reasoning mode.” If RAG was the quick answer, the
agentic workflow became the deep, deliberate problem-solving engine that
kicked in when the question was too complex for a single pass.
And eventually, that thinking mode grew into the part of the system I’m most
proud of.
Context: The Silent Villain of NL2SQL
If there was one challenge I completely underestimated, it was context.
Not SQL. Not RAG retrieval. Not even multi-agent reasoning. Just… keeping
track of what the user said earlier.
It sounds simple in theory: pass the right amount of history to the system so
each agent knows what it needs to know. But in practice, it felt like trying to
balance a stack of dominoes during an earthquake. Too much context, and
everything spirals into noise. Too little, and the system forgets what it was
doing.
There was a stretch where my application, for reasons known only to itself,
was passing 40+ messages into the model when it genuinely only needed
three. The extra messages didn’t just increase cost — they confused the
entire workflow, causing agents to latch onto irrelevant details or produce
completely strange reasoning steps.
Figure 3: Context explosion in action
And the root cause?
My incomplete understanding of how state updates actually worked in
LangGraph.
Reducers, overwrite rules, merging behavior… all the subtle mechanics that
determine what gets passed forward, what gets trimmed, and what silently
sneaks through. It took me longer than I’d like to admit to realize that my
logic for updating state — especially message lists — was doing almost the
opposite of what I intended.
The turning point came only after stepping away from the laptop entirely. I
spent an entire day at a whiteboard, mapping everything out from scratch
and talking through every transition out loud — while my friend, who
definitely had other work to do, was more or less drafted into listening.
Somewhere in that long, slightly chaotic monologue, the pieces finally
clicked: how LangGraph reducers manage state, how updates get merged,
and what “just the right amount” of context should look like for each agent.
Once that understanding landed, the entire architecture finally made sense
in a way it never had before.
The quiet moral here: sometimes the deepest bugs aren’t technical — they’re
conceptual. And sometimes, all you need is a whiteboard and a very patient friend
who can tolerate your chaos.
The Bugs That Nearly Broke My Spirit(But Taught Me the Most)
Every engineering project has its fair share of bugs, but this one had a
certain… personality. The kinds of issues that don’t just slow you down —
they push you to understand things you didn’t know you needed to learn.
1. TheContext Explosion Bug
For a long stretch, the system insisted on passing far more messages to the
LLM than necessary — sometimes dozens — which inflated token usage and
disrupted the agentic reasoning process. It took a lot of digging,
experimenting, and slowly untangling how LangGraph handles reducers and
state merges before things finally fell into place.
2. SQL Dialect Wars
My server only accepted T-SQL, while GPT confidently produced MySQL￾style queries as if that were the only dialect in the universe.
Bridging that gap required building a layer around:
sanitizing SQL
converting dialects
programmatic pagination
domain-specific constraints
edge-case safety checks
It sometimes felt less like coding and more like translating between two
languages that refuse to agree on even basic grammar.
3. The Early Failure Mode: “Sometimes Works, Sometimes
Explodes”
In the early days, consistency was the biggest challenge. The same query
could work beautifully one moment and completely collapse the next. There
was no single dramatic fix — just a long, careful process of strengthening
domain rules, improving prompts, tightening sanitization, and giving the
model clearer boundaries.
The Deep Technical Learnings
1. I learned RAG properly — notjusttheoretically.
I finally understood how embeddings capture meaning, how vector search
impacts retrieval quality, how schema tokens influence accuracy, and why
good example queries matter so much.
2. I learned agentic reasoning from scratch.
Tools, workflows, validation loops, message routing — all the behind-the￾scenes mechanics that tutorials often gloss over.
3. I learned SSE because users hate waiting silently.
What is SSE (Server-Sent Events)?
SSE is a web standard that lets a server push updates or event-data to a client
over a single, long-lived HTTP connection — instead of the client repeatedly
asking “any new data?” the server pushes new data as soon as it’s available.
In practice, this means you can stream real-time feedback, progress updates
or partial results to users — exactly what I needed when the agentic pipeline
sometimes took 20+ seconds. Without SSE, users would just stare at a blank
screen and assume the app crashed. Streaming kept the interaction feeling
alive and responsive.
4. I learned how importantitis to handle vague questions
carefully.
Early on, the system struggled with queries that didn’t specify enough
context or intent. Trying to guess what the user “might” mean almost always
led to inaccurate results. It’s far safer — and ultimately more helpful — to ask
for clarification when a question is too broad or underspecified.
Where NL2SQL Is Going (In My Opinion)
I don’t think the future is simply “convert natural language to SQL.”
I think NL2SQL will become:
“talk to your data like you talk to a data engineer.”
You’ll ask things like:
“Why did sales dip last month?”
“Show anomalies in customer behavior.”
“Forecast next quarter’s numbers.”
“Explain what went wrong last year.”
And the system won’t just return tables — it’ll return insights.
That’s the direction I want this system to evolve toward:
more human, more contextual, more insightful, and faster.
Final Thoughts
This project taught me more than I expected:
How RAG works under the hood
How agentic systems come together
How fragile context can be
How to design pipelines that feel conversational but remain reliable
I started by building a simple RAG-based NL2SQL module.
I ended up building a hybrid engine:
metadata-aware
intent-classifying
RAG-powered
agentic-reasoning
SSE-streaming
and production-ready
It wasn’t smooth. It wasn’t linear.
But it was one of the most rewarding engineering journeys I’ve had.
And this is just version one.
